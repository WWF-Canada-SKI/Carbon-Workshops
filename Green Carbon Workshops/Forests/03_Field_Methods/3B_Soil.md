## What you're collecting, and why

Soil carbon is not predicted from a dimension the way tree carbon is. It is **measured
directly**, which means physical samples and a lab bill. For every depth increment you need
three numbers:

| Input | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| **Depth interval** (cm) | Measured in the field — top and bottom of each slice |
| **Dry bulk density** (g/cm³) | A known volume of soil, oven-dried and weighed |
| **Organic carbon** (%) | The lab — by LOI₅₅₀ or elemental analysis |

Multiply them and you have carbon per unit area for that slice. Sum the slices and you have the
core.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **This is the destructive step, and it goes last.** Every vegetation survey in the plot must
> be finished first, and on a permanent plot the cores go **outside** the vegetation plot
> boundaries. See the [ordering rule](README.md#-the-order-matters-more-than-anything-else-on-this-page).

## Step 1 — Survey the depth before you sample

*How deep is the soil, and where is a representative spot?*

This is the step teams skip, and it is the one that decides whether a single core means
anything.

<table>
<tr>
<td width="55">

**Across the site.** Using a soil probe or auger, measure the **depth to substratum** — bedrock,
till, or whatever the soil sits on — at every intersection of a **10–25 m grid** across the
site. Run a tape in one direction, take a measurement every 10–25 m, shift over, repeat.

**Within the plot.** At each plot, take a depth measurement at every **1 m intersection of the
10 × 10 m plot** — 100 measurements. It goes quickly with a probe and two people.

**Then choose your coring spot**: somewhere at or near the plot's *average* depth, not the
deepest or shallowest corner.

</td>
<td width="45%">

> 📸 **[PHOTO NEEDED]** — a soil probe inserted to refusal, with the depth being read off.

> 📸 **[FIGURE NEEDED]** — the 1 m grid across a 10 × 10 m plot with depths recorded at each
> intersection, and the chosen coring location marked.

</td>
</tr>
</table>

> [!TIP]
> **Why this matters so much.** Soil carbon stock is roughly `depth × bulk density × %C`. Depth
> is the term that varies most across a plot — a stony ridge can go from 60 cm to 10 cm in a few
> metres. A core taken at a deep pocket and scaled across the plot will overestimate badly. The
> depth survey is what makes one core defensible.

**📋 Record it:** the plot's average depth to substratum goes on the `2. Core Log`.

## Step 2 — Choose a sampling method

Three methods, in order of preference:

<table>
<tr>
<td width="33%">

**A · Soil core** *(preferred)*

A corer driven into the ground, recovering an intact column in a plastic sleeve.

✅ Cleanest depth control
✅ Known volume for bulk density
❌ Hard in stony or very dry soil

</td>
<td width="33%">

**B · Soil pit**

A pit dug to expose the profile face, sampled by horizon with a punch and a bulk-density ring.

✅ Lets you see and describe horizons
✅ Works where a corer won't penetrate
❌ Slow, and very destructive

</td>
<td width="33%">

**C · Shallow soils**

For thin soils over bedrock — common on the Shield.

✅ The only option where soil is a few cm deep
❌ Small volumes, higher relative error

</td>
</tr>
</table>

See the [Non-peat guide](../../_Shared/Non-peat-FINAL-Eng-2026.pdf), *Sampling*, for the full
step-by-step of each. The
[soil checklist](checklists/Soil_Carbon_Skill_Checklist.docx) covers all three.

## Step 3 — Take the core

*How do I get an intact column with a known volume?*

<table>
<tr>
<td width="45%">

> 📸 **[PHOTO NEEDED]** — driving the corer, and the recovered core in its sleeve alongside the
> open hole.

</td>
<td width="55%">

1. Clear the surface litter to a defined starting point, and note what you removed.
2. Drive the corer to your target depth, or to refusal.
3. **Measure the depth of the hole** and **the length of the core recovered**. These are two
   different numbers and you need both.
4. Turn the corer horizontally, slide a plastic sleeve around the core, and cap it.
5. Label with the **Core ID** and keep it cool and upright.

</td>
</tr>
</table>

### Compaction: the two numbers, and what they do and don't change

As a corer is driven in, soil compresses. You recover a 44 cm core from a 50 cm hole.

```
compaction factor = hole depth ÷ core length recovered
```

The [Non-peat guide](../../_Shared/Non-peat-FINAL-Eng-2026.pdf) is explicit that **the hole
depth is the true depth of the core**. The core length is what sits in the tube.

> [!NOTE]
> **What compaction does and does not affect.** The tube holds exactly the soil that came out of
> the hole — no more, no less. So **the total carbon in the core is unaffected** by compaction.
>
> What *is* affected is **depth attribution**. A slice sitting 20–25 cm down the tube actually
> came from deeper than 20–25 cm in the ground. So any stock reported *to a fixed depth* — your
> 30 cm reporting depth, say — is biased by compaction, while the full-core stock is not.
>
> The calculator computes the compaction factor for you and **flags any core over 1.05**, with
> exactly this caveat. Reduce compaction where you can: a sharp, bevelled corer edge and a
> gentle twisting motion cut through the root mat instead of forcing it down.

**📋 Record it:** `2. Core Log` — Core ID, Plot ID, coordinates, sampling method, **hole depth**,
**core length recovered**, depth to substratum, photo series.

## Step 4 — Section, describe and bag

*How do I slice it, and what do I write down?*

<table>
<tr>
<td width="55%">

Lay the core out and cut it into **depth increments**. Common intervals are 5 cm near the
surface and 10 cm deeper, where carbon changes more slowly. Depths run **continuously** — the
bottom of one slice is the top of the next.

For each slice, record:

- **Top and bottom depth** (cm)
- **Coarse fragments** — the volume per cent of stones over 2 mm
- **Colour, texture, horizon** — and anything notable: a root mat, a charcoal layer, an abrupt
  transition to mineral soil

Bag each slice separately, labelled with **Core ID and depth interval**, and keep it cool.

</td>
<td width="45%">

> 📸 **[PHOTO NEEDED]** — a core laid out with a tape alongside and the slice boundaries marked.

> 📸 **[PHOTO NEEDED]** — labelled bags showing the `Core ID · top–bottom cm` convention.

</td>
</tr>
</table>

### Coarse fragments: the correction nobody makes

<table>
<tr>
<td width="55%">

**Rocks hold no carbon.** A soil that is 30% stone by volume holds roughly 30% less carbon per
unit volume than the same soil without stones — and much of the Canadian Shield is stony.

Neither WWF guide's scaling equations correct for this, so **estimates from stony sites come
out too high** unless you handle it. Estimate the coarse fragment volume per cent for each
slice in the field, and record it.

</td>
<td width="45%">

> 📸 **[PHOTO NEEDED]** — a sieved sample with the >2 mm fraction separated out beside the fine
> earth.

</td>
</tr>
</table>

> [!WARNING]
> **The one way to get this badly wrong is to correct for stones twice.** Labs report bulk
> density on one of two bases, and they are not interchangeable:
>
> | Basis | Meaning | Correction |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Fine earth ÷ total volume** | Mass of the <2 mm fraction, divided by the whole sample volume | Stones are **already accounted for**. No further correction |
> | **Fine earth ÷ fine-earth volume** | Mass of the <2 mm fraction, divided by only the volume it occupies | Multiply by `(1 − coarse fragment %)` |
>
> The calculator has a **Bulk density basis** column on `5. Soil Data` with exactly these two
> options, and applies the right arithmetic for each. **Ask your lab which one they report** and
> set the column accordingly. Getting it wrong understates a stony site by as much as a third.

## Step 5 — Get it to the lab

Samples should be kept cool, then dried and processed as
[Part 4](../04_Data_Interpretation/) describes. Contact the lab **before** you go to the field —
handling, packaging and minimum sample mass all vary, and it is far easier to meet their
requirements than to re-collect.

**📋 Record it:** `5. Soil Data`, one row per slice. The blue columns wait for the lab.
