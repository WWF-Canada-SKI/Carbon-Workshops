# 3A — Peat Coring

*The main event. Survey the depth, take the core, read it, and package it without losing the
one thing that makes it worth anything — knowing which end was up and how deep each bit came
from.*

**Source:** [*Measuring Carbon in Peat Soils*](Peat-FINAL-Eng-2026.pdf) (WWF-Canada, 2024),
with corer selection and recording requirements from
[Bansal et al. (2023)](../../_Shared/s13157-023-01722-2.pdf) pp.16–22.

**Fills:** `1. Plot & Site Log` · `2. Core Log` · `3. Peat Data`

> ⚠ **Read [the overview](README.md) first** — the ordering rule, the photo series, the CoreID
> convention and the safety section all apply here and are not repeated.

---

## The five stages

| | Stage | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | [**Depth survey**](#stage-1--the-depth-survey) | A depth at every grid node; a coring point |
| **2** | [**Extract the core**](#stage-2--extract-the-core) | 50 cm of peat in a barrel, drive by drive |
| **3** | [**Reveal, measure, photograph**](#stage-3--reveal-measure-and-photograph) | Core length, stratigraphy notes, von Post, a photo |
| **4** | [**Package or section**](#stage-4--package-whole-or-section-in-the-field) | Bagged sections, or a wrapped whole core |
| **5** | [**Record it properly**](#stage-5--what-goes-on-the-sheet) | A `2. Core Log` row that the calculator can use |

## Stage 1 — The depth survey

You did the site-level survey in [Part 2, Step 5](../02_Project_Planning/README.md#step-5--decide-where-the-cores-go).
This is the **within-plot** survey, and it decides where the corer goes in.

### How

Probe at every **1 m intersection** in the **10 × 10 m** plot — **121 points**. The
[peat guide](Peat-FINAL-Eng-2026.pdf) gives two ways:

<table>
<tr>
<td width="50%">

**Method A — digital grid**

Overlay a 1 × 1 m grid on the plot before you go, and record depth at every intersection.

Works well with a tablet and a mapped plot. Needs decent GNSS to find the nodes, which under a
swamp canopy you may not have.

</td>
<td width="50%">

**Method B — two tapes** *(more robust)*

1. Run one tape along the plot edge, end to end (10 m)
2. Run a second tape perpendicular to it, across the plot
3. Probe at each metre along the second tape
4. Move the second tape one metre along the first, and repeat
5. Continue until all 121 intersections are probed

</td>
</tr>
</table>

### Reading the probe

Push the probe down until it meets firm resistance, then read the depth at the peat surface.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Resistance is not always the mineral contact, and this is the single most common source of
> bad depth data.** A probe can stop on:
>
> - **Buried wood** — very common in swamps and in any peatland that was forested in the past
> - A **dense, woody or highly humified layer** part-way down the profile
> - A **gravel or sand lens** above the true base
>
> and it can also **punch through** soft, saturated mineral material and overstate depth.
>
> **Push, feel, then confirm.** Wood gives a hard, abrupt stop that often shifts if you move 20 cm
> sideways. The mineral contact gives a firmer, gritty resistance that is consistent across
> neighbouring probes. If one probe reading disagrees sharply with its neighbours, re-probe
> beside it before believing it.
>
> The definitive answer comes from the core. That is why `Bore depth` and
> `Depth to mineral contact` are **separate columns** in `2. Core Log`.

### Choosing the coring point

The guide's rule: select the coring spot as **"a representation of the entire plot — typically
average depth of the area, free from obstructions."**

**Use the median of your 121 probe depths, not the mean.** A single very deep reading — a hole
between hummocks, or a probe that punched into soft mineral — should not drag the target. Find a
point at or near the median depth, and core there.

Then move off it only for a physical reason, and **write the reason in the notes**:

- A tree, root mass or surface log
- Standing water too deep to work in
- Ground too unstable to stand on

> [!TIP]
> **Keep the 121 depths.** They are not scratch numbers. They give you the plot's depth
> distribution, they justify your coring point to a reviewer, and pooled across plots they are the
> **variability prior** for your next campaign
> ([Part 2, Step 4](../02_Project_Planning/README.md#the-depth-survey-is-your-variability-prior)).
> A photo of a filled-in grid on waterproof paper is enough.

> 📸 **[FIGURE NEEDED]** — a filled 11 × 11 depth grid for one plot, with the median contour and
> the chosen coring point marked, beside a photo of the two-tape layout in the field.

## Stage 2 — Extract the core

### The corer, and its two positions

A **Macaulay** or **Russian** corer is a **side-filling** corer: a semi-cylindrical barrel with a
pivoting guard, extension rods and a handle. It does **not** work like a push tube.

**Assemble it:** attach at least one extension rod to the barrel (screw or pin), then the handle
on top. **Add roughly one more rod per additional metre of depth.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Find and mark the closed position before you go to the field.** The peat guide's test:
>
> | Serrated / sharpened edge sits… | Position |
> |---|---|
> | On the **inside edge of the guard** | **OPEN** — the barrel will fill |
> | In the **middle of the guard** | **CLOSED** — the sample is sealed in |
>
> **Mark "closed" on the handle with tape or paint.** You will be judging this by feel, in the
> rain, with cold hands, at the end of 3 m of rod. Getting it wrong means an empty barrel and a
> wasted drive.

### The 50 cm drive sequence

The corer collects **50 cm at a time**. A full profile is built from sequential drives:

```
Drive 1  →   0 – 50 cm
Drive 2  →  50 – 100 cm
Drive 3  → 100 – 150 cm
   ...        ...
Drive n  →  until REFUSAL — clay, rock, or an obstruction
```

**Refusal** is the point at which the corer can no longer be pushed into the medium. It is the
end of the core, and *why* it happened is a data point — see
[Stage 5](#stage-5--what-goes-on-the-sheet).

### Step by step

> **Crew: three to four people.** This is the guide's specification and it is not padding.

| # | Do | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | With the corer **OPEN**, align it with the coring point | As vertical as you can get it. A leaning corer overstates depth and shears the sample |
| **2** | Push down until the **top of the barrel is level with the top of the interval** you are collecting | **Do not overshoot.** Past the intended depth and the sample is invalid — you have material from below mixed in |
| **2a** | Dense peat may need two or three people pushing together, or a sledgehammer | **Tape the target depth on the rods** before you start. It is the only reliable stop |
| **3** | Turn the handle **180°**. The barrel carves through the peat and meets the guard — now **CLOSED** | One smooth rotation. Reversing part-way mixes the sample |
| **4** | Lift from the handle, keeping **rotational pressure** on so the barrel stays against the guard | Let the pressure off and the sample slides out underground |
| **5** | A second person lifts **low on the rods**, close to the ground, ready to catch and **clasp barrel and guard together** as it emerges | This is the moment most samples are lost |
| **6** | Keep it **straight** coming out. If it sticks, **rock it gently** to break the suction | Do not lever it sideways — you will bend the rods |
| **7** | Once clear of the ground, **keep pressing barrel against guard** | |
| **8** | Turn the corer **horizontal**, sample resting on the guard, barrel facing **up** | |
| **9** | Carry it **horizontal** to the tarp | A tilted core drains and slumps |

> 📸 **[FIELD PHOTOS NEEDED]** — the corer in open and closed positions, side by side with the
> serrated edge visible; the two-person lift at step 5; a core being carried horizontally.

### ⚠ Compaction works differently here than in a push corer

This is the most important conceptual difference from the
[Forests soil protocol](../../Forests/03_Field_Methods/3B_Soil.md), and getting it wrong
introduces a systematic error into every stock you report.

<table>
<tr>
<td width="50%">

**A push corer** (Forests) drives an open tube down into the soil. The column inside **compresses**
under the load. You recover a 30 cm column from a 35 cm hole, and the fix is arithmetic: a
**compaction factor** that stretches the measured lengths back out.

</td>
<td width="50%">

**A side-filling corer** (here) rotates a chamber **sideways** into undisturbed peat. Nothing is
pushed ahead of it, so there is very little to compress —
[Bansal et al.](../../_Shared/s13157-023-01722-2.pdf) Table 4 lists **"minimal compaction"** as its
main advantage.

**So the Forests compaction arithmetic does not apply, and applying it would inflate your stocks.**

</td>
</tr>
</table>

**The failure modes here are different ones:**

| Failure | What it looks like | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Incomplete chamber filling** | Recovered length shorter than the drive | **Material is missing**, not compressed. You cannot stretch it back — there is nothing to stretch |
| **Smearing** | A streaked, blurred contact where a sharp one should be | The blade dragged material along the barrel wall. Common in wet, fluid fen peat. The depths are wrong by a few cm either side of the smear |
| **Surface compaction** | The peat surface pushed down around the corer before extraction | Real, and it *is* worth recording — see below |

**Hence the `Recovery ratio` in `2. Core Log`, not a compaction factor:**

$$\text{Recovery ratio} = \frac{\text{core length recovered}}{\text{bore depth}}$$

The calculator flags anything below **0.9** (`QC_RECOVERY_MIN`). In the
[worked example](../Worked_Example/), core `MB-03-C1` recovered 184 cm from a 214 cm bore — a ratio
of **0.86** — and the flag says exactly the right thing:

> *"Recovered only 86% of the bore depth — the chamber probably did not fill completely. Material
> is missing, which is not the same as compaction."*

A low-recovery core is still usable for **carbon density** (the sections you have are real), but
its **total stock is a minimum**. Say so.

### The four numbers Bansal says to record

[Bansal et al.](../../_Shared/s13157-023-01722-2.pdf) p.22 lists the "crucial information to collect
during the process of sampling." All four are columns in `2. Core Log`:

| # | Measurement | Definition | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Core length recovered** | Total length of peat actually in the barrel | The numerator of the recovery ratio |
| **2** | **Surface compaction** | The difference between the surface elevation of the inserted corer just before extraction and the **true soil surface** | Tells you the top of your core is not the top of the profile. Usually 0–2 cm in peat; larger on a soft floating mat |
| **3** | **Bore depth** | The depth to which the corer was inserted | The denominator of the recovery ratio, and your depth reference |
| **4** | **Cylinder diameter** | Barrel internal diameter, cm | **Needed for sample volume, and therefore for bulk density.** A number people forget because it never changes — record it once per corer and copy it down |

> [!WARNING]
> **Cylinder diameter and chamber length are how bulk density gets its volume.** A
> semi-cylindrical barrel of diameter $d$ and section length $L$ gives a half-cylinder of volume
>
> $$V = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)^{2} L$$
>
> Get $d$ wrong by 10% and **every bulk density is out by 21%**, and so is every carbon stock.
> Measure the barrel with calipers once, write it on the corer in permanent marker, and put it on
> the sheet for every core.
>
> **If the lab computes bulk density for you, ask which volume basis they used** and whether they
> assumed a full cylinder or a half cylinder. This is a real and easy-to-miss factor-of-two error.

## Stage 3 — Reveal, measure and photograph

The core is out, in the barrel, on the tarp. Now read it — this is the only time anyone will see
the intact stratigraphy.

### Reveal it

1. Lay the corer on the tarp, **barrel facing up**
2. Keeping it **horizontal**, one person holds barrel and guard and opens the barrel, turning the
   corer to reveal the sample
3. A second person holds the handle and turns while the first helps the sample free. You may need
   to lift the corer off the ground — **keep it horizontal**
4. Once fully revealed, lay it flat on the tarp

### Measure and describe

**Measure the core length** (cm), then note every distinct change in the profile. The guide's
list:

| Record | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Colour changes** | Green → brown; brown → black; a reddish band |
| **Texture changes** | Fibrous → mushy → greasy |
| **Whether changes are gradual or distinct** | A sharp contact means something happened fast — a fire, a flood, a hydrological shift |
| **Visibly large objects** | **Wood**, rocks, roots, identifiable plant remains. Note the depth of each |
| **Missing areas or gaps** | Where the barrel did not fill, or material fell out |
| **Water saturation** | Mucky, semi-saturated, dry |

**And two more this workshop adds**, because neither is in the WWF guide and both matter:

#### von Post humification

Squeeze a handful of wet peat and watch what comes out between your fingers. Subjective, rapid,
and the standard peatland descriptor for two centuries.

| Class | Decomposition | What you see when you squeeze it |
|---|---|---|
| **H1** | Completely undecomposed | Clear, colourless water. Plant remains easily identifiable |
| **H2** | Almost undecomposed | Clear, yellowish water. Plant remains still identifiable |
| **H3** | Very weakly decomposed | Slightly turbid brown water. **No peat** escapes between the fingers |
| **H4** | Weakly decomposed | Strongly turbid brown water. No peat escapes. Residue mushy |
| **H5** | Moderately decomposed | Very turbid water, **a little peat** escaping. Plant structure evident but indistinct |
| **H6** | Fairly well decomposed | **About one third** escapes. Residue very mushy |
| **H7** | Strongly decomposed | **About half** escapes. Plant structure barely discernible |
| **H8** | Very strongly decomposed | **About two thirds** escapes. Only resistant remains — roots, fibres |
| **H9** | Almost completely decomposed | Nearly all escapes as a fairly uniform paste |
| **H10** | Completely decomposed | **All** escapes. No free water visible |

**H1–H3 = fibric peat (Oi) · H4–H6 = hemic, "mucky peat" (Oe) · H7–H10 = sapric, "muck" (Oa).**

Record von Post **per section**, or at minimum at each distinct change plus at the base. The
`von Post at base` column in `2. Core Log` is the one that tells you whether you reached what you
think you reached: a base reading of H8–H10 next to mineral material is a contact; H3 at refusal
is probably buried wood.

*The same table is in the calculator's `R1. Reference` tab.*

#### The transitions to look for — Frames A, B and C

The [peat guide](Peat-FINAL-Eng-2026.pdf)'s appendix gives three worked examples of what to note.
They are the three transitions you will meet most often:

| Frame | Where | The note that matters |
|---|---|---|
| **A** — surface core, *Sphagnum* bog | 0–50 cm | **The depth where green, living *Sphagnum* turns to brown peat.** This is the vegetation/peat boundary, and [3B](3B_Vegetation.md) needs it |
| **B** — mid-core | 50–100 cm | **Dark mucky peat at the top grading to lighter peat below**, and the depth of any **significant wood** |
| **C** — the base | at the contact | **The depth of the peat → mineral transition**, the **texture and colour** of the mineral layer, and the depth where **coarse fragments** become common |

*Photo credit for the guide's Frame A/B/C images: Cathal Doherty.*

> 📸 **[FIELD PHOTOS NEEDED]** — the Frame A/B/C series at higher resolution for this page, plus a
> revealed core laid out on the tarp with the whiteboard in frame.

### Photograph it

1. Write **site and core number** on the whiteboard
2. Place it beside the core
3. Photograph **from directly above**, with the **entire core profile and the whiteboard in frame**
4. **No shadows across the core** — shade the whole thing or none of it

A photographed core is recoverable data. A core with no photo and ambiguous notes is not.

## Stage 4 — Package whole, or section in the field

**Decide this before you leave home**, because the equipment differs.

| | **Section in the field** | **Package whole** |
|---|---|---|
| **Time in field** | Longer — 20–40 min per core | Shorter |
| **Time in lab** | Shorter | Longer, and you still have to do it |
| **Risk** | Mislabelled bags | The core slumps, smears or dries in transit and the depths blur |
| **Best when** | You have crew and daylight, and you want **fine sections for dating** | Short on time, poor weather, or a long walk out |
| **The guide's advice** | | *"If short on time, transport the whole peat core to the lab for subsequent sectioning and processing"* |

### Option A — Section in the field

**One section cut and packaged at a time.** Never cut the whole core and then bag it.

| # | Step |
|---|---|
| **1** | Work **top down** — surface end first, then down the core |
| **2** | **Cut at distinct layers** wherever you can, so each section is a homogeneous mass |
| **3** | Sections **at least 1 cm, no more than 5 cm** long |
| **4** | Cut with a **serrated knife** |
| **5** | Keep the knife where it cut, and use a **trowel** to transfer the section into its **pre-labelled** resealable bag |
| **6** | Straight into the **cooler** |
| **7** | **Wash and dry** knife and trowel |
| **8** | Repeat to the base of the core |
| **9** | Into a **freezer** as soon as possible |
| **10** | Clean the corer and all tools thoroughly |

> [!TIP]
> **Two people, two jobs.** The guide is explicit: one person sections, one labels bags with
> **CoreID, section number and depth interval**. A single person doing both is how a 70-bag core
> ends up with two bags labelled "40–45".

**Label every bag with the depth interval, not just the section number.** `MB-01-C1 · S14 ·
65–70 cm` survives a cooler flooding and a smudged marker; `#14` does not.

### Option B — Package the whole core

| # | Step |
|---|---|
| **1** | Check the PVC pipe and poster-board cutouts are labelled **TOP** and **BOTTOM** |
| **2** | Line the PVC pipe with **aluminium foil**, then **plastic wrap** |
| **3** | Place the pipe over the core, **top label at the surface end** |
| **4** | **Flip corer and pipe together** so the core falls into the pipe cradle — at least two people |
| **5** | If the sample sticks, free it with a serrated knife or flat blade. **Wash the blade between sections** |
| **6** | Set the corer aside |
| **7** | Wrap the sample in **plastic wrap then aluminium foil**, still tracking top and bottom |
| **8** | Lay **poster board** over it |
| **9** | **Duct tape** the poster board on |
| **10** | Label with the **CoreID** in permanent marker |
| **11** | Keep **horizontal**, poster board up, into an empty cooler |
| **12** | Pack the cooler so **nothing moves** in transit |
| **13** | Into a **freezer** as soon as possible |
| **14** | Clean the corer and tools |

> [!WARNING]
> **"Top" and "bottom" is the whole game.** A core with its orientation lost is worthless — every
> depth is unknown, so every section's position in the profile is unknown, and an inverted core
> produces an age-depth model that runs backwards. Label **both ends**, on **both** the pipe and
> the poster board, before the core goes anywhere near it.

### If you are dating the core

[Part 5 — Chronology](../05_Chronology_Supplement/) needs more than stock work does, and the
difference is all in the sectioning:

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **1–2 cm continuous sections** | Through the **upper 10–50 cm**. 5 cm sections smear the ¹³⁷Cs peak away and it cannot be recovered |
| **Continuous, not spot samples** | The CRS ²¹⁰Pb model **requires the entire inventory**. A gap invalidates every date below it |
| **Section to supported background** | Keep going until ²¹⁰Pb activity flattens — you will not know where that is in the field, so **section finely deeper than you think you need** |
| **Avoid metal contamination** | Bansal p.22 notes radionuclide and trace-element work "requires additional care to avoid contamination" (IAEA 2003; Brenner & Kenney 2013). Use a clean stainless or ceramic blade for dating sections, and wash between every one |
| **Keep a ¹⁴C target from the base** | **Terrestrial seeds, leaves or twigs.** Not roots (they grew down from above — too young) and not aquatic macrofossils (reservoir effect — too old). Bag them separately and note the depth |

## Stage 5 — What goes on the sheet

One row per core in `2. Core Log`, one row per section in `3. Peat Data`.

### `2. Core Log` — the fields that carry judgement

| Field | Enter | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `Drives taken` | Count of 50 cm drives | Your cross-check on total depth |
| `Bore depth (cm)` | How deep the corer went | |
| `Core length recovered (cm)` | How much peat came out | With bore depth, gives the recovery ratio |
| `Surface compaction (cm)` | Bansal's measurement 2 | Usually small; record it anyway |
| `Depth to mineral contact (cm)` | Leave **blank** if you did not reach it | **Do not enter the bore depth as a proxy** |
| `Reached mineral contact?` | **Yes / No** | The single most consequential field on the sheet |
| `von Post at base` | H1–H10 | Corroborates the field above |
| `Chamber length / diameter (cm)` | From the corer | Sets sample volume |
| `Photo series?` | Yes / No | |
| `Notes` | **Why refusal happened** | "Refusal on buried wood at 104 cm"; "hit gravel"; "rods bending, stopped" |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **`Reached mineral contact? = No` changes what your number means.** A core that stopped short
> gives a **minimum** stock, not a total. The calculator flags it and the flag is not a nag:
>
> In the [worked example](../Worked_Example/), `CS-02-C1` hit refusal on buried wood at 104 cm:
>
> > *"Core did not reach the mineral contact — the full-profile stock is a MINIMUM, not the whole
> > store."*
>
> **What to do about it:** re-core 1–2 m away. Buried wood is local; the contact is not. Two
> attempts at a plot is normal in a swamp. If three attempts all refuse at a similar depth, you
> have probably found a real woody layer — record that as a finding and report the stock as a
> minimum.

### `3. Peat Data` — one row per section

| Field | Source |
|---|---|
| `Core ID`, `Section no.`, `Top (cm)`, `Bottom (cm)` | Your labels. **Top and bottom, not thickness** — the calculator derives thickness and checks for gaps and overlaps |
| `Section thickness`, `Volume` | Calculated |
| `von Post` | Field |
| `Bulk density (g/cm³)` | Lab |
| `Water content %` | Lab |
| `LOI / organic matter %` | Lab |
| `Carbon % (measured)` | Lab, if you ran CHN. Leave blank to use LOI × factor |
| `Notes` | Wood, gaps, drive boundaries |

> [!TIP]
> **Flag the drive boundaries in the notes.** The contact between drive 1 and drive 2 (at 50 cm,
> 100 cm and so on) is where smearing and material loss concentrate. A section that straddles a
> drive boundary is the section most likely to be wrong, and a note now saves an argument later.

## Common failure modes, and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| **Barrel comes up empty** | Corer was in the wrong position, or rotated back part-way | Check your closed-position mark. Re-drive 30 cm away |
| **Recovery ratio well below 0.9** | Chamber did not fill — often loose fibrous surface peat, or very fluid fen peat | Note it. Consider a **Wardenaar box corer** for the surface. Do **not** apply a compaction correction |
| **Refusal much shallower than the probe said** | Buried wood or a stone | Re-core 1–2 m away. Note both attempts |
| **Refusal much deeper than the probe said** | Probe stopped on a hard layer part-way down | Believe the core. This is why probe depth and core depth are separate columns |
| **Core smeared, contacts blurred** | Blade dragging in wet fluid peat | Note the affected depth range. Sections there carry extra uncertainty |
| **Surface peat falls apart on reveal** | Normal for H1–H2 *Sphagnum* | Section it *in the barrel* if you can. Or use a box corer |
| **Rods bending under load** | Dense peat or an obstruction | **Stop.** Bent rods make depth unreliable and the corer unsafe. Record refusal and move |
| **Water flooding the hole** | Normal in a fen | Work faster, keep the barrel closed until it is on the tarp |
| **Core warmed up in the vehicle** | Delay getting to a freezer | Note it. Warm, wet peat respires — carbon content shifts. Coolers with ice packs, and freeze the same day |
